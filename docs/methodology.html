Methodology

Every model, written out

Aimed at a reader with bachelor's-level probability and statistics. Each section answers four questions: what is the object, why it is needed, how it is estimated or computed, and what impact it has on the final result.

1 · Two models of how prices move

1.1 Geometric Brownian motion — the Black–Scholes world

The baseline model says the price \(S_t\) has a constant expected growth rate and constant noise, proportional to the current level:

$$dS_t = \mu\, S_t\, dt + \sigma\, S_t\, dW_t$$

where \(W_t\) is Brownian motion, \(\mu\) is the annual drift and \(\sigma\) the annual volatility. By Itô's lemma, log-returns over a step \(\Delta t\) are i.i.d. Gaussian:

$$\ln\frac{S_{t+\Delta t}}{S_t} \sim \mathcal N\!\Big(\big(\mu - \tfrac{1}{2}\sigma^2\big)\Delta t,\; \sigma^2 \Delta t\Big)$$
WhyGBM is the assumption underneath the Black–Scholes formula. If the desk prices with Black–Scholes, it is implicitly betting that returns are Gaussian — no crashes, no fat tails.
HowSimulated with the exact log-Euler step \(S_{t+\Delta t}=S_t\exp[(\mu-\sigma^2/2)\Delta t+\sigma\sqrt{\Delta t}\,Z]\), \(Z\sim\mathcal N(0,1)\).
ImpactEvery "matched world" experiment and the desk's believed world in the mismatch experiments run on this process.

1.2 Merton jump-diffusion — a world that can gap

Merton (1976) adds crashes: on top of the diffusion, jumps arrive as a Poisson process with intensity \(\lambda\) per year, and each jump multiplies the price by \(e^{Y}\) with \(Y \sim \mathcal N(\mu_J, \sigma_J^2)\):

$$\frac{dS_t}{S_{t^-}} = (\mu - \lambda k)\,dt + \sigma\, dW_t + \big(e^{Y}-1\big)\,dN_t, \qquad k = \mathbb E[e^{Y}]-1 = e^{\mu_J + \sigma_J^2/2}-1$$

The \(-\lambda k\,dt\) term is a compensator: it recentres the process so \(\mu\) remains the total expected growth rate. A daily log-return is then a Poisson mixture of Gaussians — with probability \(e^{-\lambda \Delta t}(\lambda \Delta t)^n/n!\) the day contains \(n\) jumps, and conditional on that the return is Gaussian with mean and variance shifted by \(n\mu_J\) and \(n\sigma_J^2\). Mixing Gaussians of different variances is exactly what produces fat tails and skew.

WhyReal index returns have far more extreme days than a Gaussian allows (SPY's worst day in-sample is −11.6%, a ≈9σ event under the fitted GBM). A desk that ignores this underprices crash risk.
HowSimulated by adding \(N\sim\text{Poisson}(\lambda\Delta t)\) jumps per step with total log-size \(\mathcal N(N\mu_J,\, N\sigma_J^2)\), minus the compensator.
ImpactThe "true world" of the steamroller experiment, with parameters taken from the COVID-era option chain — not invented.

2 · Pricing the option under each model

2.1 Black–Scholes

Under GBM with risk-free rate \(r\), the European call price is

$$C = S_0\Phi(d_1) - K e^{-rT}\Phi(d_2), \qquad d_{1} = \frac{\ln(S_0/K) + (r + \sigma^2/2)T}{\sigma\sqrt{T}},\quad d_2 = d_1 - \sigma\sqrt T$$

with \(\Phi\) the standard normal CDF; the put follows from put–call parity. The hedge ratio ("delta") is \(\partial C/\partial S = \Phi(d_1)\) for a call and \(\Phi(d_1)-1\) for a put.

2.2 Merton

Conditional on \(n\) jumps occurring during the option's life, the terminal log-price is still Gaussian — so the Merton price is a Poisson-weighted average of Black–Scholes prices with jump-adjusted inputs:

$$C_{\text{Merton}} = \sum_{n=0}^{\infty} \frac{e^{-\lambda' T}(\lambda' T)^n}{n!}\; C_{BS}\big(S_0, K, T;\; r_n,\, \sigma_n\big),\qquad \lambda' = \lambda(1+k)$$ $$\sigma_n^2 = \sigma^2 + \frac{n \sigma_J^2}{T}, \qquad r_n = r - \lambda k + \frac{n\,(\mu_J + \sigma_J^2/2)}{T}$$

The series is truncated at 60 terms (the neglected Poisson tail is smaller than \(10^{-40}\) for any parameters used here). The Merton delta is the same weighted average applied to Black–Scholes deltas.

WhyBecause option payoffs are convex, fat tails raise option values: for the same diffusive \(\sigma\), Merton prices sit above Black–Scholes — the market charges for crash insurance.
HowBoth pricers implement a common interface (price, delta), so the desk can believe one model while the world runs another.
ImpactThe gap between the two prices at the same inputs is the mispricing per contract the steamroller desk gives away on every fill.

3 · Calibration I — the physical measure (what returns actually do)

3.1 GBM by maximum likelihood

With Gaussian log-returns the MLE is available in closed form: the sample mean and variance of daily log-returns map to

$$\hat\sigma = \sqrt{\hat v / \Delta t}, \qquad \hat\mu = \frac{\hat m}{\Delta t} + \frac{\hat\sigma^2}{2},$$

where \(\hat m, \hat v\) are the sample mean and (MLE) variance. Standard errors follow from the exact Fisher information of a Gaussian sample — \(\mathrm{Var}(\hat m)=v/n\), \(\mathrm{Var}(\hat v)=2v^2/n\) — pushed through the transformations by the delta method. A key honest disclosure: with 17 years of data, \(\hat\sigma\) is estimated to three decimals, but \(\hat\mu\) carries a standard error of about 4.5% per year — drift is nearly unlearnable from daily data, which is precisely why hedged option P/L (which is drift-free) matters.

3.2 Merton by numerical maximum likelihood

The daily-return density is the Poisson mixture

$$f(x) = \sum_{n=0}^{8} \frac{e^{-\lambda\Delta t}(\lambda\Delta t)^n}{n!}\; \varphi\!\Big(x;\; \big(\mu - \tfrac{\sigma^2}{2} - \lambda k\big)\Delta t + n\mu_J,\; \sigma^2\Delta t + n\sigma_J^2\Big)$$

maximised numerically over \((\mu,\sigma,\lambda,\mu_J,\sigma_J)\) with positivity enforced by log-parameterisation, multi-start Nelder–Mead followed by BFGS polish (the likelihood is multi-modal — jumps and diffusion can partially trade off). Standard errors come from the observed information: the inverse of the numerically-differentiated Hessian of \(-\ln L\) at the optimum, mapped through the log-links by the delta method.

3.3 Which model wins? Likelihood ratio, AIC, BIC

$$LR = 2\big(\ell_{\text{Merton}} - \ell_{\text{GBM}}\big), \qquad AIC = 2k - 2\ell, \qquad BIC = k\ln n - 2\ell$$

The models are nested (Merton \(\to\) GBM as \(\lambda \to 0\)), so a large \(LR\) is evidence against GBM. One subtlety stated openly: the null hypothesis \(\lambda = 0\) sits on the boundary of the parameter space, so the usual \(\chi^2_3\) reference distribution is conservative. With \(LR \approx 1627\) (SPY) and \(1313\) (NIFTY) against a 95% critical value of 7.8, the conclusion survives any reasonable correction.

Why"Use a jump model" should be a conclusion from data, not an aesthetic choice. This section proves the desk's Gaussian belief is rejected by four decades of combined market history.
Howmrlab/calibrate.py; unit tests confirm the estimator recovers known parameters on synthetic data before it touches real data.
ImpactThe fitted decomposition — e.g. SPY: 9.8% diffusive vol plus ≈88 small jumps/year of mean −0.26% — feeds the QQ and density figures, and grounds the whole "the world jumps" premise.

4 · Calibration II — the risk-neutral measure (what options cost)

Option prices reveal the market's model. Quoting all options in implied-volatility units — the \(\sigma\) that makes Black–Scholes reproduce a price — a Gaussian world would give a flat line across strikes. Real chains show a skew: out-of-the-money puts carry far higher implied vols. We fit Merton parameters to four real SPY chains (≈90 days to expiry) by weighted least squares on the smile:

$$\min_{\sigma,\lambda,\mu_J,\sigma_J}\; \sum_i w_i \big(IV_{\text{Merton}}(K_i) - IV_{\text{mkt}}(K_i)\big)^2, \qquad w_i \propto \exp\!\Big[-\tfrac{1}{2}\big(\tfrac{\ln(K_i/S)}{0.15}\big)^2\Big]$$

ATM-centred weights reflect that at-the-money quotes are the most informative per unit of noise (highest vega). For speed the objective uses the first-order equivalence \(\Delta IV \approx \Delta \text{price}/\text{vega}\) — vega evaluated once at market IVs — while the reported fit quality is computed by exact inversion. Only OTM quotes with positive bids enter (the liquid side of each smile).

WhyThe steamroller's "true world" should be a world someone actually priced. On 16 March 2020 the market paid for \(\lambda = 3.7\) crashes/year with mean size −31%: those are the parameters the mismatched desk faces.
HowImplied vols by Brent root-finding on Black–Scholes; arbitrage-violating quotes dropped; multi-start simplex with box constraints.
ImpactFour fitted smiles with RMSE 0.7–1.3 vol points — a 4-parameter model explaining 40+ vol-point skews — and the scenario parameters for every desk experiment.

5 · The desk — how market-making P/L is generated

Every tick the desk quotes a fresh option with constant maturity \(T\) = 3 months and strike at the current spot (constant moneyness keeps the business stationary). Around its believed fair value \(V_{\text{model}}(S_t)\) it posts

$$\text{bid} = V_{\text{model}} - s, \qquad \text{ask} = V_{\text{model}} + s$$

with half-spread \(s\). When a client trades, the contract's P/L is realised on the spot: the underlying is simulated over the option's life under the true model, while the desk delta-hedges with its own model's delta, financing at \(r\); premium, hedge cash flows and terminal payoff are discounted back.

5.1 Why a hedged option earns "premium minus fair value"

For a delta-hedged position rebalanced continuously under the model's own dynamics, the random \(dW\) term cancels and the hedged portfolio grows at the risk-free rate — so discounted P/L converges to

$$\text{P/L} \;\longrightarrow\; \text{premium} - V_{\text{fair}},$$

independent of the drift \(\mu\). Selling at \(V+s\) or buying at \(V-s\) therefore banks \(+s\) per contract in expectation — if \(V\) is the true fair value and the hedge is the true delta. Model error breaks both at once: quotes centre on the wrong value (systematic negative edge) and the hedge misses jumps entirely (a jump moves the price a finite amount in an instant; no continuous rebalancing can follow it). Two honest caveats are quantified in the report: discrete hedging (30 rebalances) leaves mean-zero noise, and under jumps the market is incomplete, so even the right-model desk keeps some undiversifiable jump risk and a small drift exposure.

5.2 Elastic client flow — mispricing attracts sharks

Real clients compare quotes to value. Each side's fill probability is scaled by how bad the quote is for the client, measured in half-spreads against true fair value:

$$p_{\text{fill}} = \min\Big(0.98,\; p_0 \cdot e^{-\eta\,\text{excess}}\Big), \qquad \text{excess}_{\text{ask}} = \frac{\text{ask} - V_{\text{truth}}}{s} - 1$$

A fairly-priced quote trades at the base rate; a rich quote loses business; a cheap quote gets run over (capped at \(e^{2}\times\) — client arrival is finite). Flow is put-heavy (clients net-buy protection), so the mispriced desk ends up systematically short crash insurance — the classic real-world position.

WhyWithout elasticity, an over-pricing desk looks brilliant (clients overpay forever). With it, both directions of mispricing are punished — which is what adverse selection does in real markets.
Howmrlab/simulate.py: seeded NumPy generators, every experiment reproducible to the penny; unit tests pin the matched desk's edge to the quoted half-spread across seeds.
ImpactThe three tracked curves — realised P/L, edge the desk believes (vs own fair), true expected edge (vs true fair) — make model risk visible as the gap between belief and truth.

6 · Risk metrics — pricing the damage

Each scenario is run over hundreds of independent seeds, giving a Monte-Carlo sample of one-year desk P/L \(\{L_i\}\). From it:

$$\text{VaR}_\alpha = -q_\alpha(L), \qquad \text{ES}_\alpha = -\,\mathbb E\big[L \,\big|\, L \le q_\alpha(L)\big]$$

Value-at-Risk is the loss threshold exceeded with probability \(\alpha\); Expected Shortfall is the average loss beyond it. ES is reported alongside VaR because it is the number that actually distinguishes a fat-tailed desk from a thin-tailed one with the same quantile (and it is subadditive — coherent).

6.1 The model-risk premium

Re-running the whole desk over a grid of half-spreads gives the loss-probability curve \(s \mapsto \mathbb P(L(s) < 0)\). The break-even spread is the smallest \(s\) with \(\mathbb P(L(s)<0) \le 5\%\). The matched desk breaks even at 8.8 cents. The mispriced desk does not reach 5% anywhere below $15 — because with elastic flow, widening quotes throttles the business faster than it buys safety. The difference is a dollar price of running the wrong model.

7 · Validation — catching the wrong model from P/L alone

This is the model-validator's view: treat the desk's model as a hypothesis and test it against realised outcomes.

7.1 Kupiec's proportion-of-failures test

A correct 5% VaR model is exceeded on 5% of days. Observing \(x\) exceptions in \(n\) days:

$$LR_{\text{pof}} = -2\ln\!\left[\frac{(1-\alpha)^{n-x}\alpha^{x}} {(1-\hat\pi)^{n-x}\hat\pi^{x}}\right] \;\sim\; \chi^2_1 \quad\text{under } H_0,\qquad \hat\pi = x/n$$

The matched desk: 83 exceptions in 1,500 ticks (expected 75), \(p = 0.35\) — correct coverage. The mispriced desk: 811 exceptions, \(p < 10^{-16}\).

7.2 Christoffersen's independence test

Coverage alone is not enough — exceptions must not cluster. Modelling the exception indicator as a two-state Markov chain with transition counts \(n_{ij}\) and testing \(\pi_{0} = \pi_{1}\):

$$LR_{\text{ind}} = -2\ln\frac{(1-\hat\pi)^{n_{00}+n_{10}}\hat\pi^{\,n_{01}+n_{11}}} {(1-\hat\pi_0)^{n_{00}}\hat\pi_0^{\,n_{01}}(1-\hat\pi_1)^{n_{10}}\hat\pi_1^{\,n_{11}}} \sim \chi^2_1, \qquad LR_{cc} = LR_{\text{pof}} + LR_{\text{ind}} \sim \chi^2_2$$

Clustered exceptions are the signature of missing dynamics (jumps, volatility regimes) — exactly what the mispriced desk shows.

7.3 Bootstrap inference on the realised edge

Is the desk's edge statistically positive? Seeds are i.i.d. by construction, so a percentile bootstrap on per-seed average edge gives an honest confidence interval: matched desk \(+0.153\), 95% CI \([0.146, 0.159]\), against a promised \(0.15\) — delivered. The steamroller: \(-10.20\), CI \([-10.62, -9.78]\) — while its own model reports \(+0.15\).

7.4 CUSUM — the early-warning monitor

A validator does not wait a year. The one-sided CUSUM tracks cumulative under-delivery of the promised edge (spot-scaled residuals, control limits calibrated on a burn-in window):

$$C_0 = 0,\qquad C_t = \max\big(0,\; C_{t-1} + (m - X_t) - k\big), \qquad \text{alarm when } C_t > h$$

with allowance \(k\) = 0.5σ and threshold \(h\) = 8σ. On the mispriced desk it alarms at trade 83 of 1,467 — roughly three weeks into a six-year run — while never false-alarming on the matched desk in the same configuration.

WhyThe lesson of the project: model risk is not just a loss — it is a detectable statistical signal, and the validator's job is to detect it before the steamroller arrives.
Howmrlab/validate.py, tested against synthetic series with known properties (correct coverage accepted, wrong coverage rejected, clustering flagged, under-delivery alarmed).
ImpactA complete monitoring playbook a bank's model-risk team could run on any desk's daily P/L, demonstrated end-to-end on this one.
