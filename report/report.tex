\begin{abstract}
\noindent
A market maker earns the bid--ask spread by quoting around \emph{fair value} ---
but fair value comes from a model, and the model can be wrong. This document is a
self-contained account of a project that measures the cost of that error in
dollars and then detects it statistically. It is written for a reader with
bachelor's-level mathematics and statistics and assumes nothing beyond that:
Brownian motion, the Poisson process, It\^o's lemma, option pricing,
maximum-likelihood theory, the likelihood-ratio/AIC/BIC machinery, Value-at-Risk
and Expected Shortfall, the Kupiec and Christoffersen backtests, the bootstrap and
the CUSUM monitor are each built up from first principles before they are used.
Headline findings: on 17+ years of SPY and NIFTY~50 daily returns the Gaussian
(Black--Scholes) model is rejected against the Merton jump-diffusion with
likelihood-ratio statistics of $\approx 1627$ and $\approx 1313$; on 16 March 2020
the SPY options market priced $3.7$ crashes per year of average size $-31\%$; a
simulated desk that prices with the matched model realises $+0.153$ per contract
against a promised $0.15$ (bootstrap 95\% CI $[0.146,0.159]$), while the same desk
pricing Black--Scholes in the jump world \emph{believes} $+0.15$ and realises
$-10.20$; no half-spread below \$15 makes the wrong-model desk 95\%-safe, versus
8.8 cents for the right one; and a validation suite catches the broken model at
trade 83 of 1{,}467 with zero false alarms on the healthy desk.
\end{abstract}

\section{Random variables, moments, and why tails matter}

\subsection{Expectation, variance, and higher moments}
A random variable $X$ has expectation (mean) $\E[X]$, the probability-weighted
average of its outcomes, and variance
\begin{equation}
\Var(X)=\E\big[(X-\E X)^2\big],\qquad \text{sd}(X)=\sigma_X=\sqrt{\Var(X)} .
\end{equation}
Mean and variance describe location and spread, but two distributions can share
both and still be utterly different \emph{in the tails} --- and finance lives and
dies in the tails. Two standardised moments capture the difference. Writing
$Z=(X-\E X)/\sigma_X$:
\begin{equation}
\text{skewness}=\E[Z^3],\qquad
\text{excess kurtosis}=\E[Z^4]-3 .
\end{equation}
Skewness measures asymmetry (negative skew: the long tail is on the loss side).
Excess kurtosis measures tail weight relative to the normal distribution, whose
$\E[Z^4]$ is exactly 3 --- hence the ``$-3$''. Daily equity returns typically show
negative skew and large positive excess kurtosis; a Gaussian has zero of both.

\subsection{The normal distribution and the size of its tails}
The normal (Gaussian) law $\N(\mu,\sigma^2)$ has density
\begin{equation}
\varphi(x;\mu,\sigma^2)=\frac{1}{\sigma\sqrt{2\pi}}
\exp\!\Big[-\frac{(x-\mu)^2}{2\sigma^2}\Big].
\end{equation}
It appears everywhere because of the \textbf{central limit theorem}: sums of many
small independent shocks are approximately Gaussian. That is exactly the logic
behind Gaussian models of returns --- a day's return is the sum of thousands of
small trades. The logic fails when \emph{one} shock is occasionally enormous
(a crash): then the sum is dominated by a single term and the CLT approximation
breaks in the tails. How thin are Gaussian tails? For $Z\sim\N(0,1)$:
\begin{center}
\small
\begin{tabular}{lccccc}
\toprule
$k$ & 2 & 3 & 5 & 7 & 9\\
$\Prob(Z<-k)$ & $2.3\times10^{-2}$ & $1.3\times10^{-3}$ & $2.9\times10^{-7}$
& $1.3\times10^{-12}$ & $1.1\times10^{-19}$\\
``once every'' & 2 months & 3 years & 13{,}000 yrs & $3\times10^{9}$ yrs &
$3\times10^{16}$ yrs\\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{center}
(``once every'' converts the probability to trading days.) Keep this table in
mind: Section~\ref{sec:physobs} shows SPY's worst in-sample day is a
$\approx\!9\sigma$ event under the fitted Gaussian --- an event that model says
should not happen in the age of the universe, yet it is right there in the data.

\section{Brownian motion}
\label{sec:bm}

\subsection{From coin flips to a continuous process}
Take a symmetric random walk: every time step $\Delta t$, move up or down by
$\sqrt{\Delta t}$ with probability $\tfrac12$ each. Over time $t$ there are
$t/\Delta t$ steps, so the position has mean 0 and variance
$(t/\Delta t)\cdot\Delta t=t$. Letting $\Delta t\to0$, the CLT turns the position
at time $t$ into a Gaussian $\N(0,t)$, and the limiting object is
\textbf{Brownian motion} $W_t$, characterised by:
\begin{enumerate}[itemsep=1pt]
\item $W_0=0$;
\item independent increments: $W_t-W_s$ is independent of everything up to $s$;
\item Gaussian increments: $W_t-W_s\sim\N(0,\,t-s)$;
\item continuous paths.
\end{enumerate}

\subsection{The one strange rule: \texorpdfstring{$(\dd W)^2=\dd t$}{(dW)\texttwosuperior{} = dt}}
Over a short interval $\dd t$ the increment $\dd W$ has standard deviation
$\sqrt{\dd t}$, which is \emph{much larger} than $\dd t$ itself when $\dd t$ is
small. Consequently the squared increment $(\dd W)^2$ has mean $\dd t$ and
negligible variance around it: to first order in $\dd t$,
\begin{equation}
(\dd W)^2 = \dd t \qquad\text{(the quadratic-variation rule).}
\label{eq:qv}
\end{equation}
This is the single fact that makes stochastic calculus different from ordinary
calculus, and it is the origin of every ``$\tfrac12\sigma^2$'' term below.

\subsection{It\^o's lemma}
Let $X_t$ follow $\dd X=a\,\dd t+b\,\dd W$ and let $f(x)$ be smooth. A Taylor
expansion of $f(X_{t+\dd t})$ keeps terms up to second order, because by
\eqref{eq:qv} the second-order term $(\dd X)^2=b^2\dd t$ is \emph{first order in
time}:
\begin{equation}
\dd f
= f'(X)\,\dd X+\tfrac12 f''(X)\,(\dd X)^2
= \Big(a f'(X)+\tfrac12 b^2 f''(X)\Big)\dd t+b f'(X)\,\dd W .
\label{eq:ito}
\end{equation}
This is \textbf{It\^o's lemma} --- the chain rule with one extra term
$\tfrac12 b^2 f''$, the price of infinitely wiggly paths.

%======================================================================
\section{The Poisson process}
\label{sec:poisson}

Crashes do not arrive on a schedule; they arrive \emph{at random times}. The
canonical model for random arrivals is the \textbf{Poisson process} $N_t$ with
intensity $\lambda$ (events per year):
\begin{enumerate}[itemsep=1pt]
\item $N_0=0$, and $N_t$ counts events up to time $t$;
\item independent increments;
\item in a short interval $\dd t$: exactly one event with probability
  $\lambda\,\dd t$, more than one with probability negligible compared to
  $\dd t$.
\end{enumerate}
From these axioms the count over any interval of length $t$ is Poisson
distributed:
\begin{equation}
\Prob(N_t=n)=e^{-\lambda t}\frac{(\lambda t)^n}{n!},\qquad
\E[N_t]=\Var(N_t)=\lambda t .
\label{eq:poispmf}
\end{equation}
Waiting times between events are exponential with mean $1/\lambda$ and
memoryless: a market that has not crashed for five years is (in this model) no
``safer'' than yesterday. Section~\ref{sec:merton} attaches a random \emph{size}
to each arrival to build the jump part of the Merton model.

%======================================================================
\section{Geometric Brownian motion: the Black--Scholes world}
\label{sec:gbm}

\subsection{The model}
The standard model for an asset price $S_t$ says that \emph{percentage} changes
have constant drift and constant noise:
\begin{equation}
\dd S_t=\mu S_t\,\dd t+\sigma S_t\,\dd W_t .
\label{eq:gbmsde}
\end{equation}
$\mu$ is the expected growth rate per year, $\sigma$ the volatility per year.
Percentage (not absolute) changes, because a \$1 move means something different
at $S=10$ than at $S=1000$.

\subsection{Solving it, and the origin of \texorpdfstring{$-\tfrac12\sigma^2$}{-sigma\texttwosuperior/2}}
Apply It\^o's lemma \eqref{eq:ito} to $f(S)=\ln S$ (so $f'=1/S$,
$f''=-1/S^2$), with $a=\mu S$ and $b=\sigma S$:
\begin{equation}
\dd \ln S=\Big(\frac{\mu S}{S}-\tfrac12\frac{\sigma^2S^2}{S^2}\Big)\dd t
+\frac{\sigma S}{S}\dd W
=\Big(\mu-\tfrac{\sigma^2}{2}\Big)\dd t+\sigma\,\dd W .
\end{equation}
Integrating from 0 to $t$:
\begin{equation}
S_t=S_0\exp\!\Big[\big(\mu-\tfrac{\sigma^2}{2}\big)t+\sigma W_t\Big],
\qquad
\ln\frac{S_{t+\Delta t}}{S_t}\sim
\N\!\Big(\big(\mu-\tfrac{\sigma^2}{2}\big)\Delta t,\;\sigma^2\Delta t\Big).
\label{eq:gbmsol}
\end{equation}
So under GBM, \textbf{daily log-returns are i.i.d.\ Gaussian}. That is the
falsifiable statement the data will judge in Part~\ref{part:estimation}. The
$-\sigma^2/2$ is not a convention; it is forced by It\^o's lemma and reflects
the gap between the average of growth rates and the growth rate of the average.
The simulator uses \eqref{eq:gbmsol} directly (the ``exact log-Euler'' step), so
path simulation carries no discretisation error.

\subsection{What GBM asserts about the world}
No jumps (paths are continuous); constant volatility; Gaussian tails (recall the
tail table in \S1.2); returns independent day to day. Each assertion can fail in
reality; this project focuses on the most dangerous failure for an options desk
--- the missing jumps --- because a delta hedge can, in principle, track a
continuous path but can \emph{never} track a gap.

\section{The Merton jump-diffusion: a world that can gap}
\label{sec:merton}

\subsection{Construction}
Superimpose on GBM a stream of jumps: arrivals from a Poisson process $N_t$ with
intensity $\lambda$ (\S\ref{sec:poisson}); at each arrival the price is
multiplied by $e^{Y}$ with $Y\sim\N(\mu_J,\sigma_J^2)$ drawn independently.
Between jumps the price diffuses as GBM. The SDE is
\begin{equation}
\frac{\dd S_t}{S_{t^-}}
=(\mu-\lambda k)\,\dd t+\sigma\,\dd W_t+\big(e^{Y}-1\big)\dd N_t ,
\qquad
k=\E\big[e^{Y}\big]-1=e^{\mu_J+\sigma_J^2/2}-1 .
\label{eq:mertonsde}
\end{equation}
The value of $k$ uses the lognormal-mean identity
$\E[e^{Y}]=e^{\mu_J+\sigma_J^2/2}$ for Gaussian $Y$ --- a computation worth doing
once by completing the square in
$\int e^{y}\varphi(y;\mu_J,\sigma_J^2)\dd y$.

\textbf{The compensator.} Jumps with negative mean would drag the expected
growth rate below $\mu$; the term $-\lambda k\,\dd t$ (the \emph{compensator})
subtracts the expected jump contribution so $\mu$ remains the total expected
growth rate. Without it, comparing ``GBM with drift $\mu$'' against ``Merton
with drift $\mu$'' would compare two different expected returns and the desk
experiments would be confounded.

\subsection{The distribution of a daily return: a Poisson mixture of Gaussians}
Condition on the number of jumps $n$ in the day. Given $n$, the log-return is
Gaussian --- diffusion contributes mean $(\mu-\sigma^2/2-\lambda k)\Delta t$ and
variance $\sigma^2\Delta t$; the $n$ i.i.d.\ jump sizes add mean $n\mu_J$ and
variance $n\sigma_J^2$. Weighting by the Poisson probabilities
\eqref{eq:poispmf}:
\begin{equation}
f(x)=\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}e^{-\lambda\Delta t}\frac{(\lambda\Delta t)^n}{n!}\;
\varphi\!\Big(x;\;\big(\mu-\tfrac{\sigma^2}{2}-\lambda k\big)\Delta t+n\mu_J,\;
\sigma^2\Delta t+n\sigma_J^2\Big).
\label{eq:mertondensity}
\end{equation}
A \textbf{mixture of Gaussians with different variances is not Gaussian}: the
high-variance components dominate far from the centre, producing fat tails, and
$\mu_J<0$ shifts those components left, producing negative skew. This is the
entire mechanism by which the extra parameters $(\lambda,\mu_J,\sigma_J)$ buy
realistic tails.

\section{Options and arbitrage-free pricing}
\label{sec:options}

\subsection{Contracts and payoffs}
A \textbf{European call} with strike $K$ and expiry $T$ pays
$\max(S_T-K,0)$; a \textbf{put} pays $\max(K-S_T,0)$. A put is crash
insurance: it pays exactly when the market has fallen below $K$. ``Moneyness''
$K/S$ locates the strike relative to spot (an at-the-money (ATM) option has
$K\approx S$; an out-of-the-money (OTM) put has $K<S$).

\subsection{No-arbitrage and put--call parity}
An \textbf{arbitrage} is a free lunch --- a trade with no initial cost, no risk
of loss, and positive probability of gain. Pricing proceeds by ruling these
out. The cleanest example: hold (long call, short put, same $K,T$). At expiry
this pays $\max(S_T-K,0)-\max(K-S_T,0)=S_T-K$ in every state. A position paying
$S_T-K$ for sure must cost its discounted certain value today:
\begin{equation}
C-P=S_0-Ke^{-rT}
\qquad\text{(put--call parity)},
\label{eq:parity}
\end{equation}
where $r$ is the risk-free interest rate. Parity is model-free --- it holds no
matter how $S$ moves --- which makes it a perfect \emph{unit test}: our pricer
satisfies it to $10^{-10}$.

\subsection{Delta hedging and the Black--Scholes argument}
Why does a \emph{model} give a unique price at all? Black and Scholes's insight:
under GBM the option's risk can be \emph{eliminated} by trading the underlying.
Let $V(S,t)$ be the option value. By It\^o's lemma,
\begin{equation}
\dd V=\Big(V_t+\mu S V_S+\tfrac12\sigma^2S^2V_{SS}\Big)\dd t
+\sigma S V_S\,\dd W .
\end{equation}
Form a portfolio: long the option, short $\Delta=V_S$ shares. Its change is
$\dd V-V_S\,\dd S$, and the $\dd W$ terms \emph{cancel exactly}:
\begin{equation}
\dd V - V_S\dd S=\Big(V_t+\tfrac12\sigma^2S^2V_{SS}\Big)\dd t .
\end{equation}
A riskless portfolio must earn the risk-free rate $r$ (else arbitrage), giving
the \textbf{Black--Scholes PDE}
\begin{equation}
V_t+\tfrac12\sigma^2S^2V_{SS}+rSV_S-rV=0 ,
\label{eq:bspde}
\end{equation}
whose solution with terminal condition $\max(S-K,0)$ is the
\textbf{Black--Scholes formula}
\begin{equation}
C_{BS}=S_0\Phi(d_1)-Ke^{-rT}\Phi(d_2),\quad
d_1=\frac{\ln(S_0/K)+(r+\sigma^2/2)T}{\sigma\sqrt T},\quad
d_2=d_1-\sigma\sqrt T ,
\label{eq:bs}
\end{equation}
$\Phi$ the standard normal CDF; the put follows from parity \eqref{eq:parity}.
Two facts to notice. \emph{First}, $\mu$ has vanished: because the hedge removes
directional risk, the price cannot depend on expected return --- only on
volatility. \emph{Second}, the argument needs continuous paths (to rebalance
$\Delta$ continuously) and a known constant $\sigma$; both fail in a jump world,
which is precisely the vulnerability this project quantifies. The hedge ratios
used throughout are $\Delta_{\text{call}}=\Phi(d_1)$,
$\Delta_{\text{put}}=\Phi(d_1)-1$, and
``vega'' $=\partial V/\partial\sigma=S\sqrt{T}\,\varphi(d_1)$ (needed in
\S\ref{sec:rncalib}).

\subsection{Risk-neutral pricing in one paragraph}
\label{sec:rn}
The PDE argument is equivalent to a probabilistic recipe: price as if the world
were risk-neutral --- replace $\mu$ by $r$ in the dynamics, then take the
discounted expected payoff, $V=e^{-rT}\E^{\,Q}[\text{payoff}]$. The measure $Q$
under which this works is called the \emph{risk-neutral measure}. The practical
upshot used later: \textbf{option prices reveal the market's probabilistic
model} (under $Q$) --- fitting a model to option prices recovers the dynamics
the \emph{market} is pricing, as opposed to fitting returns, which recovers the
dynamics the world actually follows (the \emph{physical} measure $P$).

\section{Pricing under Merton: a series of Black--Scholes prices}
\label{sec:mertonpricing}

Under the risk-neutral Merton dynamics, condition on the number of jumps $n$
during the option's life (probability
$e^{-\lambda' T}(\lambda' T)^n/n!$ with $\lambda'=\lambda(1+k)$ --- the
jump-size tilt $1+k$ arises from the measure change). Given $n$ jumps, the
terminal log-price is Gaussian, so each conditional expectation is a
Black--Scholes price with adjusted inputs, and the total price is the weighted
sum --- \textbf{Merton's series}:
\begin{equation}
C_M=\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\frac{e^{-\lambda' T}(\lambda' T)^n}{n!}\,
C_{BS}(S_0,K,T;\,r_n,\sigma_n),\qquad
\sigma_n^2=\sigma^2+\frac{n\sigma_J^2}{T},\quad
r_n=r-\lambda k+\frac{n(\mu_J+\sigma_J^2/2)}{T}.
\label{eq:mertonseries}
\end{equation}
Interpretation of the adjustments: $n$ jumps add $n\sigma_J^2$ of log-variance
(spread over the life $T$, hence the $/T$), and shift the risk-neutral growth by
their total expected log-size, with the compensator $-\lambda k$ keeping the
whole package arbitrage-free. Implementation truncates at 60 terms; since the
weights are Poisson, the neglected tail is bounded by
$\Prob(N>60)<10^{-40}$ for every parameter set used here. The Merton delta is
the same weighted average of Black--Scholes deltas.

\begin{keybox}
\textbf{Why $C_M>C_{BS}$ at equal diffusive $\sigma$.} An option payoff is
convex. By Jensen's inequality, spreading the terminal distribution wider (fat
tails) raises the expected payoff. The dollar gap
$C_M-C_{BS}$, evaluated at calibrated parameters, is the \emph{mispricing per
contract} a Black--Scholes desk gives away in a jump world --- it will reappear
in Part~\ref{part:desk} as roughly \$10 per contract.
\end{keybox}

\subsection{Implied volatility and the smile}
\label{sec:iv}
Quoting prices in dollars makes options with different strikes incomparable.
The market convention is to quote each option by its \textbf{implied
volatility}: the unique $\sigma$ that makes \eqref{eq:bs} reproduce the price
(unique because the BS price is strictly increasing in $\sigma$; we invert
numerically with Brent's root-finder, discarding quotes that violate
no-arbitrage bounds). If the world were GBM, every strike would imply the
\emph{same} $\sigma$: a flat line. Real markets display a \textbf{skew/smile}:
OTM puts imply much higher volatilities. Under a jump model this is exactly
what should happen --- crash risk inflates the value of low-strike puts
specifically. The smile is thus a diagnostic plot for model error, read
directly off market data in \S\ref{sec:rnobs}.

\section{Statistical machinery from scratch}
\label{sec:statmachinery}

\subsection{Likelihood and maximum likelihood}
Given data $x_1,\dots,x_n$ assumed i.i.d.\ from a density
$f(x;\theta)$ with unknown parameter vector $\theta$, the \textbf{likelihood} is
the joint density read as a function of $\theta$:
\begin{equation}
L(\theta)=\prod_{i=1}^n f(x_i;\theta),\qquad
\ell(\theta)=\ln L(\theta)=\sum_{i=1}^n\ln f(x_i;\theta).
\end{equation}
The \textbf{maximum-likelihood estimator (MLE)} $\hat\theta$ maximises
$\ell$: it is the parameter value under which the observed data were most
probable. Under regularity conditions the MLE is consistent (converges to the
truth) and asymptotically normal:
\begin{equation}
\hat\theta\;\approx\;\N\big(\theta,\; I(\theta)^{-1}\big),\qquad
I(\theta)=-\E\Big[\frac{\partial^2\ell}{\partial\theta\,\partial\theta^{\top}}\Big]
\quad\text{(Fisher information)}.
\label{eq:mleasymp}
\end{equation}
In practice $I$ is estimated by the \emph{observed information} --- the negative
Hessian of $\ell$ at $\hat\theta$, computed numerically --- and
\textbf{standard errors} are the square roots of the diagonal of its inverse.
Intuition: the information matrix measures how sharply peaked the likelihood is;
a sharp peak means the data pin the parameter down tightly.

\subsection{The delta method}
If $\hat\theta$ has standard error $s$ and we report a transformation
$g(\hat\theta)$, a first-order Taylor expansion gives
\begin{equation}
\text{se}\big(g(\hat\theta)\big)\approx|g'(\hat\theta)|\,s .
\label{eq:delta}
\end{equation}
We use this twice: to convert SEs of (daily mean, daily variance) into SEs of
annual $(\mu,\sigma)$, and to convert SEs of log-parameterised Merton
parameters (positivity is enforced by optimising $\ln\sigma,\ln\lambda,
\ln\sigma_J$) back to the natural scale.

\subsection{Hypothesis tests, p-values, and the likelihood-ratio test}
\label{sec:lrtest}
A \textbf{hypothesis test} confronts a null hypothesis $H_0$ (e.g.\ ``GBM is
the true model'') with data. A \emph{test statistic} is chosen whose
distribution under $H_0$ is known; the \textbf{p-value} is the probability,
if $H_0$ were true, of a statistic at least as extreme as observed. Small
p-value $\Rightarrow$ the data are implausible under $H_0$ $\Rightarrow$ reject.
Rejecting a true null (false alarm) is a \emph{type I error}, controlled at the
significance level (5\% throughout); failing to reject a false null is a
\emph{type II error}, controlled by test power.

For \textbf{nested models} (the smaller obtained from the larger by fixing
parameters --- here GBM = Merton with $\lambda=0$), \emph{Wilks's theorem} says
that under $H_0$
\begin{equation}
LR=2\big(\ell_{\text{big}}-\ell_{\text{small}}\big)\;\sim\;\chi^2_d ,
\label{eq:wilks}
\end{equation}
$d$ = number of restricted parameters (here $d=3$: $\lambda,\mu_J,\sigma_J$).
The 95\% critical value of $\chi^2_3$ is 7.81.

\textbf{A boundary caveat, stated honestly.} Wilks's theorem assumes the null
lies in the \emph{interior} of the parameter space. Our null $\lambda=0$ sits on
the boundary ($\lambda$ cannot be negative), and when $\lambda=0$ the jump-size
parameters $\mu_J,\sigma_J$ become meaningless (unidentified). Both issues push
the true null distribution of $LR$ \emph{below} $\chi^2_3$, so using
$\chi^2_3$ is \emph{conservative}: it makes rejecting harder, not easier. With
observed statistics above 1{,}300 against a critical value of 7.81, no
correction could change the verdict --- but at small samples this caveat is the
difference between careful and careless inference.

\subsection{AIC and BIC}
Adding parameters always increases the maximised likelihood; information
criteria penalise the increase to prevent overfitting:
\begin{equation}
AIC=2k-2\ell,\qquad BIC=k\ln n-2\ell ,
\end{equation}
$k$ = number of parameters. Lower is better. BIC's penalty grows with sample
size, making it the stricter criterion; a BIC difference above 10 is
conventionally called ``decisive''. We report both alongside the LR test so the
conclusion does not hinge on a single criterion.

\subsection{QQ plots}
A quantile--quantile plot is the visual counterpart of a goodness-of-fit test.
Sort the data $x_{(1)}\le\dots\le x_{(n)}$; plot $x_{(i)}$ against the model
quantile $F^{-1}\big((i-\tfrac12)/n\big)$. If the model is right the points lie
on the 45° line; systematic S-shaped departures at the ends mean the model's
tails are too thin. For Merton, $F^{-1}$ has no closed form; we compute the
mixture CDF from \eqref{eq:mertondensity} on a fine grid and invert by
interpolation.

\section{Calibration I: the physical measure (what returns actually do)}
\label{sec:physical}

\subsection{GBM: the MLE in closed form}
By \eqref{eq:gbmsol}, daily log-returns are i.i.d.\
$\N(m,v)$ with $m=(\mu-\sigma^2/2)\Delta t$, $v=\sigma^2\Delta t$. Setting
$\partial\ell/\partial m=0$ and $\partial\ell/\partial v=0$ for the Gaussian
log-likelihood yields the sample mean $\hat m$ and the (divide-by-$n$) sample
variance $\hat v$; mapping back,
\begin{equation}
\hat\sigma=\sqrt{\hat v/\Delta t},\qquad
\hat\mu=\frac{\hat m}{\Delta t}+\frac{\hat\sigma^2}{2}.
\end{equation}
Exact Gaussian sampling theory gives $\Var(\hat m)=v/n$ and
$\Var(\hat v)=2v^2/n$; the delta method \eqref{eq:delta} converts these to SEs
for $\hat\mu,\hat\sigma$.

\begin{obsbox}
\textbf{Observation (drift is unlearnable).} With $n\approx4{,}500$ days,
$\hat\sigma$ carries an SE of $\pm0.002$ --- three-decimal precision --- while
$\hat\mu$ carries an SE of $\pm4.5\%$ \emph{per year}, the same order as the
estimate itself. Two decades of daily data cannot tell a 4\% drift from a 10\%
drift. This asymmetry is structural (variance information accumulates much
faster than mean information), and it dictates a design choice made later: the
desk's P/L is built from \emph{delta-hedged} positions, whose expected value
does not depend on $\mu$ at all (\S\ref{sec:hedgedpnl}).
\end{obsbox}

\subsection{Merton: numerical MLE on the mixture density}
The log-likelihood is $\sum_i\ln f(x_i)$ with $f$ from
\eqref{eq:mertondensity}, truncated at $n\le8$ jumps per day --- justified
because even at the largest fitted $\lambda$ ($\approx88$/yr,
$\lambda\Delta t\approx0.35$), $\Prob(N>8)<10^{-10}$. Three practical
complications, each handled explicitly:
\begin{enumerate}[itemsep=2pt]
\item \textbf{Positivity}: optimise $(\mu,\ln\sigma,\ln\lambda,\mu_J,
  \ln\sigma_J)$ so the search space is unconstrained.
\item \textbf{Multi-modality}: diffusion and jumps can partially imitate one
  another (many small jumps look like diffusion), so the surface has multiple
  local maxima. We run 8 random restarts of Nelder--Mead (a derivative-free
  simplex search) and polish the best with BFGS (a quasi-Newton method).
\item \textbf{Standard errors}: the observed information --- the numerically
  differentiated Hessian of $-\ell$ at the optimum (central differences) ---
  is inverted, and the delta method maps SEs through the log-links.
\end{enumerate}
Before touching real data the estimator is validated on synthetic data with
known parameters (unit test: recovery within sampling error, and $LR>20$ when
jumps are truly present).

\section{Calibration II: the risk-neutral measure (what options cost)}
\label{sec:rncalib}

\subsection{Why fit option prices separately}
Fitting returns (\S\ref{sec:physical}) recovers the world's actual dynamics
($P$-measure). Fitting option \emph{prices} recovers the dynamics the market
uses to price risk ($Q$-measure, \S\ref{sec:rn}) --- these can differ, because
sellers of crash insurance demand compensation beyond actuarial cost. Both
matter to a desk: it hedges in the $P$-world but competes on $Q$-world prices.
We fit Merton to four real SPY chains spanning regimes: 2017-01-03 (calm),
2020-03-16 (COVID crash, VIX 82), 2022-06-13 (bear market), 2025-06-30
(recent).

\subsection{The fitting problem, in detail}
For each snapshot: take the expiry nearest 90 days to match the desk's 3-month
contract; keep OTM options only (OTM puts below spot, OTM calls above ---
the liquid side of the smile, and ITM quotes duplicate OTM information via
parity); require bid $>\$0.05$ and moneyness in $[0.70,1.30]$; compute
mid-price implied vols (\S\ref{sec:iv}). Then solve
\begin{equation}
\min_{\sigma,\lambda,\mu_J,\sigma_J}
\sum_i w_i\big(IV_M(K_i)-IV_{\text{mkt}}(K_i)\big)^2,
\qquad
w_i\propto\exp\!\Big[-\tfrac12\Big(\frac{\ln(K_i/S)}{0.15}\Big)^{\!2}\Big].
\label{eq:rnobj}
\end{equation}
\textbf{Why weight by moneyness?} An option's price sensitivity to volatility
is its vega, which peaks at the money; a fixed amount of quote noise (bid--ask
bounce) therefore corrupts ATM implied vols least. Gaussian weights centred at
ATM encode this. \textbf{Why fit IVs rather than prices?} Deep options differ
in price by orders of magnitude; a price-space objective would be dominated by
the most expensive quotes. IV-space puts all strikes on a common scale.
\textbf{A speed trick, used honestly:} a first-order Taylor expansion gives
$\Delta IV\approx\Delta\text{price}/\text{vega}$, so inside the optimiser we
minimise vega-scaled \emph{price} residuals (vega frozen at market IVs ---
a constant), avoiding a slow numerical inversion at every step; the final
reported smile and RMSE are recomputed with exact inversion. Optimisation is
multi-start Nelder--Mead with box constraints to keep the simplex out of absurd
regions.

\part{The Desk: Market-Making P/L Under Model Risk}
\label{part:desk}

%======================================================================
\section{The simulation, piece by piece}
\label{sec:desk}

\subsection{The quoting cycle}
Time advances in ticks of $dt=1/250$ years ($\approx$ one trading day). At
every tick the desk:
\begin{enumerate}[itemsep=1pt]
\item observes the spot $S_t$ (generated by the \emph{true} model --- GBM or
  Merton --- which the desk cannot see);
\item prices a \textbf{fresh} option with constant maturity $T=0.25$ and strike
  $K_t=S_t$ (rolling at-the-money) using its \emph{believed} model:
  $V_{\text{mod}}=V(S_t,K_t,T)$;
\item posts $\text{bid}=V_{\text{mod}}-s$ and $\text{ask}=V_{\text{mod}}+s$
  with half-spread $s=\$0.15$;
\item receives client flow (\S\ref{sec:flow}); each fill is realised
  immediately (\S\ref{sec:hedgedpnl}).
\end{enumerate}

\textbf{Why a rolling contract at rolling moneyness?} A desk is a
\emph{business}, not a single bet; quoting the same kind of contract every day
keeps its risk profile stationary, so P/L statistics across time are
meaningful. This was learned the hard way: an earlier version fixed the strike
$K=S_0$ forever, and over multi-year runs the drifting spot pushed the option
far out of the money --- shrinking premiums, shrinking risk, and silently
breaking the VaR backtest of \S\ref{sec:varbacktest} \emph{for the correct
model}. A validation tool caught a simulation artefact; the fix (roll the
strike with spot) is exactly the kind of specification issue model validators
exist to find.

\subsection{Why a hedged option earns ``premium minus fair value''}
\label{sec:hedgedpnl}
When a client trades, the contract's whole life is simulated immediately: the
underlying evolves under the \emph{true} model over $[0,T]$ on a 30-step grid,
and the desk delta-hedges at each step \emph{using its own model's delta},
financing at $r$. The discounted P/L of a short-at-premium-$p$ put position is
\begin{equation}
\text{P/L}=p
+\sum_{j=0}^{29}e^{-r t_j}\,h_j\big(S_{t_{j+1}}-S_{t_j}e^{r\,\delta t}\big)
-e^{-rT}\max(K-S_T,0),
\label{eq:pnlaccounting}
\end{equation}
where $h_j$ is the model-delta hedge held over $[t_j,t_{j+1}]$ (signs flip for
bought contracts and for calls). The middle term is the hedge account: you
earn the stock move but pay financing on the capital tied up, hence the
financing-correct increment $S_{t_{j+1}}-S_{t_j}e^{r\delta t}$.

\textbf{The key theorem, informally.} If the believed model is the true model
and rebalancing is continuous, the hedge term exactly cancels the randomness of
the payoff (that was the content of the Black--Scholes argument, \S6.3), and
\eqref{eq:pnlaccounting} collapses to the deterministic quantity
\begin{equation}
\text{P/L}\;\longrightarrow\;p-V_{\text{fair}} ,
\label{eq:hedgedlimit}
\end{equation}
\emph{independent of the drift $\mu$}. Selling at $p=V+s$ therefore banks
exactly $+s$; buying at $V-s$ banks $+s$ too. \textbf{Spread capture is a
theorem --- conditional on the model being right.}

\textbf{Where it breaks, precisely.}
\begin{itemize}[itemsep=2pt]
\item \emph{Discrete hedging} (30 steps, not continuous): the cancellation is
  approximate; a mean-zero noise per contract survives, shrinking like
  $1/\sqrt{\text{steps}}$. Visible as the wobble of realised P/L around the
  expected lines in Figure~\ref{fig:equity} (left).
\item \emph{Wrong fair value}: quotes centre on $V_{\text{mod}}$, so each sale
  banks $s-(V_{\text{true}}-V_{\text{mod}})$ in true expectation --- a
  systematic, sign-definite leak of about \$10/contract at COVID parameters.
\item \emph{Wrong delta + jumps}: a jump moves the price a finite amount in an
  instant; no rebalancing scheme, however frequent, can follow it. The hedge is
  simply short the jump. This is why the damage is not just ``smaller edge''
  but fat-tailed losses.
\item \emph{Incompleteness}: even the \emph{right-model} desk cannot hedge
  jumps with the underlying alone; some jump risk is unhedgeable and a small
  drift exposure survives. Observation 10 quantifies the consequence.
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Client flow: elastic, informed, put-heavy}
\label{sec:flow}
Each tick, a client may lift the ask (desk sells) with base probability 0.5 and
may hit the bid (desk buys) with base probability 0.1 --- \textbf{put-heavy
flow}, matching the real net demand for index crash protection (institutions
buy puts to insure portfolios). Crucially, clients are not price-blind. Define
the client's disadvantage in units of half-spread, e.g.\ for the ask:
\begin{equation}
\text{excess}_{\text{ask}}=\frac{\text{ask}-V_{\text{true}}}{s}-1
\qquad(\text{0 for a fairly-priced quote}),
\end{equation}
and scale the fill probability
\begin{equation}
p_{\text{fill}}=\min\!\big(0.98,\;p_0\,e^{-\eta\,\text{excess}}\big),
\qquad \eta=1,
\label{eq:elastic}
\end{equation}
with the multiplier capped at $e^{2}\approx7.4\times$ (client arrival is finite
even when the desk gives money away). Three regimes fall out naturally: a
fairly-priced desk trades at base rates; a \emph{rich} quote loses business
(clients shop elsewhere); a \emph{cheap} quote is hammered by informed flow ---
\textbf{adverse selection}. Without elasticity ($\eta=0$) the simulator
reproduces the textbook toy in which an overpricing desk looks brilliant
because clients overpay forever; with it, both directions of mispricing are
punished, which drives Observation 11.

\part{Risk Measurement: Pricing the Damage}
\label{part:risk}

%======================================================================
\section{VaR and Expected Shortfall, from scratch}
\label{sec:vares}

Let $L$ denote the desk's one-year P/L (profit positive) and $q_\alpha$ its
$\alpha$-quantile. Two standard summaries of the left tail:
\begin{equation}
\text{VaR}_\alpha=-q_\alpha(L),
\qquad
\text{ES}_\alpha=-\E\big[L\mid L\le q_\alpha(L)\big].
\label{eq:vares}
\end{equation}
\textbf{VaR} answers: ``what loss is exceeded only with probability
$\alpha$?'' ($\alpha=5\%$ or 1\% conventionally). It is a quantile ---
intuitive, but it says \emph{nothing about how bad the tail is beyond the
threshold}: two desks can share a VaR while one hides a catastrophe behind it.
\textbf{ES} fixes exactly that by averaging the losses beyond the quantile; it
is also mathematically better behaved (\emph{subadditive}: diversifying two
books cannot raise their combined ES, which can fail for VaR --- the reason
banking regulation moved market-risk capital from VaR to ES). Estimation here
is empirical: with hundreds of i.i.d.\ seeds, VaR is the sample quantile and ES
the mean of the tail sample. Both appear in Table~\ref{tab:desk}; the
steamroller's ES$_{5\%}=4{,}467$ versus the matched desk's $-4.0$ is the
risk-measurement statement of the whole project.

%======================================================================
\section{The model-risk premium: a break-even spread search}
\label{sec:breakeven}

Spread is the desk's defence: a wider $s$ banks more per trade. So --- can a
desk buy safety from a wrong model by quoting wider? Re-run the entire
steamroller and matched batches over a grid of half-spreads and estimate the
\textbf{loss-probability curve} $s\mapsto\Prob\big(\text{P/L}(s)<0\big)$; define
the break-even $s^\ast$ as the smallest $s$ with loss probability $\le5\%$
(linear interpolation between grid points). Elastic flow makes this experiment
honest: widening quotes also drives clients away, so volume falls as $s$ rises.

\begin{obsbox}
\textbf{Observation 12 (8.8 cents vs \$15+).} The matched desk crosses the 5\%
bar at $s^\ast=\$0.088$. The steamroller desk's curve
(Figure~\ref{fig:breakeven}) decays with agonising slowness --- at $s=\$15$,
more than a hundred times the matched break-even, its loss probability is
still $\approx5.8\%$, so \textbf{no half-spread below \$15 clears the bar}.
Mechanically: the desk needs $s$ to exceed the $\approx\$10$/contract
mispricing \emph{plus} compensation for jump variance, but every dollar of
extra spread throttles the arrival rate \eqref{eq:elastic}, so safety recedes
almost as fast as the desk chases it. \textbf{The model-risk premium here is
not a few cents on the spread; it is the difference between a competitive
business and no business.} A desk quoting \$15 half-spreads on a \$10 option
has no clients; the only real remedies are a better model or exiting the
product.
\end{obsbox}

\part{Validation: Catching the Wrong Model from P/L Alone}
\label{part:validation}

A model validator does not observe ``the true model''; they observe
\emph{outcomes}. This part treats the desk's model as a statistical hypothesis
and tests it against realised P/L --- the day job of bank model-risk teams
(codified in the Federal Reserve's SR~11-7 guidance). Setup for all tests: a
six-year run (1{,}500 ticks) for statistical power; and every per-tick P/L is
divided by $S_t/S_0$ --- \textbf{spot-scaling} --- because an ATM option's
dollar value is proportional to the spot level, so risk must be measured per
unit of notional for the series to be stationary. (This, too, was learned from
a failing backtest: unscaled, the matched desk's P/L variance grows with the
drifting spot and a static VaR fails coverage \emph{for the correct model} ---
a second simulation artefact caught by validation tooling.)

%======================================================================
\section{VaR backtesting}
\label{sec:varbacktest}

\subsection{The desk forecasts its own risk}
Standard practice: the desk computes VaR from \emph{its own} model. Here, the
desk simulates its believed world (Black--Scholes/GBM dynamics with its own
parameters) over 25 seeds, pools the spot-scaled per-tick P/L increments, and
takes the empirical 5\% quantile as its one-tick $\text{VaR}_{5\%}$ forecast.
Then it lives in the true world, and each tick is scored: an
\textbf{exception} occurs when the realised loss exceeds the forecast VaR.
If the model is right, exceptions should (i) occur on 5\% of ticks and (ii) be
independent over time. Kupiec tests (i); Christoffersen tests (ii).

\subsection{Kupiec's proportion-of-failures test, derived}
\label{sec:kupiec}
Under $H_0$ the exception indicator is i.i.d.\ Bernoulli($\alpha$), so the
number of exceptions $x$ in $n$ ticks is Binomial($n,\alpha$). The likelihood
of the observed sequence under exception probability $\pi$ is
$\pi^{x}(1-\pi)^{n-x}$; its MLE is $\hat\pi=x/n$. The likelihood-ratio
statistic comparing ``$\pi=\alpha$'' against ``$\pi$ free'' is
\begin{equation}
LR_{\text{pof}}
=-2\ln\frac{\alpha^{x}(1-\alpha)^{n-x}}{\hat\pi^{x}(1-\hat\pi)^{n-x}}
\;\overset{H_0}{\sim}\;\chi^2_1
\quad\text{(Wilks, \S\ref{sec:lrtest})}.
\label{eq:kupiec}
\end{equation}
Worked on our data ($n=1500$, $\alpha=0.05$, expected $75$):
\begin{itemize}[itemsep=1pt]
\item \emph{Matched desk}: $x=83$, $\hat\pi=5.53\%$. Plugging in:
  $LR_{\text{pof}}=0.87$, $p=0.351$. The observed excess of 8 exceptions is
  entirely consistent with chance. \textbf{Correct coverage.}
\item \emph{Steamroller}: $x=811$, $\hat\pi=54.1\%$. The model claimed 5\%;
  reality delivered 54\% --- the desk blows through its own VaR on more than
  half of all days. $LR_{\text{pof}}$ is in the thousands, $p<10^{-16}$.
  \textbf{Catastrophic rejection.}
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Christoffersen's independence and conditional-coverage tests}
\label{sec:christoffersen}
Correct frequency is not sufficient: 75 exceptions all in one month is a
broken model with perfect unconditional coverage. Christoffersen models the
exception indicator as a two-state Markov chain. Count transitions
$n_{ij}$ (from state $i\in\{0,1\}$ yesterday to $j$ today) and let
$\hat\pi_0=n_{01}/(n_{00}+n_{01})$, $\hat\pi_1=n_{11}/(n_{10}+n_{11})$ ---
the exception probability after a calm day vs after an exception day.
Independence means $\pi_0=\pi_1$; the LR test of that restriction is
\begin{equation}
LR_{\text{ind}}
=-2\ln\frac{(1-\hat\pi)^{n_{00}+n_{10}}\,\hat\pi^{\,n_{01}+n_{11}}}
{(1-\hat\pi_0)^{n_{00}}\hat\pi_0^{\,n_{01}}
(1-\hat\pi_1)^{n_{10}}\hat\pi_1^{\,n_{11}}}
\;\overset{H_0}{\sim}\;\chi^2_1 ,
\label{eq:christ}
\end{equation}
and the \textbf{conditional coverage} statistic
$LR_{cc}=LR_{\text{pof}}+LR_{\text{ind}}\sim\chi^2_2$ tests both properties at
once.

\begin{obsbox}
\textbf{Observation 13 (coverage).} Matched: Kupiec $p=0.351$, CC $p=0.619$
--- the correct model passes both, which certifies the tests' false-alarm
behaviour on healthy data. Steamroller: both $p<10^{-16}$.

\textbf{Observation 14 (clustering is the fingerprint of jumps).} The
steamroller's exceptions do not arrive uniformly: Figure~\ref{fig:exceptions}
shows them bunched into episodes --- a jump craters the P/L and the elevated
loss regime persists while the desk keeps quoting through it, so
$\hat\pi_1>\hat\pi_0$. Clustered exceptions tell the validator not only
\emph{that} the model is wrong but \emph{what kind} of dynamics it is missing:
state-dependent risk (jumps, volatility regimes) rather than a mere level
miscalibration of VaR.
\end{obsbox}

\section{The bootstrap: honest error bars on realised edge}
\label{sec:bootstrap}

\textbf{The problem.} ``The desk averaged $+0.153$ per contract'' --- is that
meaningfully different from the promised $0.15$? From zero? Any answer needs
the sampling distribution of the mean, and the P/L distribution is skewed and
fat-tailed, so Gaussian formulas are suspect.

\textbf{The plug-in principle.} The bootstrap replaces the unknown population
by the sample itself: draw $n$ values \emph{with replacement} from the observed
per-seed edges, compute the mean, repeat $B=10{,}000$ times; the spread of
those $B$ means estimates the sampling variability of the original mean. The
\textbf{percentile interval} takes the 2.5th and 97.5th percentiles of the
bootstrap means as a 95\% CI. Validity here is clean: seeds are i.i.d.\ by
construction, which is exactly the bootstrap's core assumption. A one-sample
$t$-test of $H_0{:}$ mean $=0$ is reported alongside as a cross-check.

\begin{obsbox}
\textbf{Observation 15.} Matched: $+0.153$, CI $[0.146,0.159]$ --- excludes
zero decisively, contains the promised $0.15$: the desk delivers its quoted
edge and the theory of \S\ref{sec:hedgedpnl} is confirmed \emph{with error
bars}, not by eyeball. Steamroller: $-10.20$, CI $[-10.62,-9.78]$ ---
significantly, catastrophically negative. Right-model: CI $[0.064,0.324]$ ---
positive but wide, quantifying how much harder it is to measure edge in a
fat-tailed world (similar seed count, several times the uncertainty).
\end{obsbox}

\section{CUSUM: the early-warning monitor}
\label{sec:cusum}

\subsection{The problem: detect a change, fast, without crying wolf}
The backtests above are retrospective --- they need a long sample. A desk
bleeding edge should be caught in \emph{weeks}. This is sequential change
detection: monitor a stream $X_1,X_2,\dots$ (here: spot-scaled per-trade edge
residuals, $X_t=$ realised edge $-$ promised edge) and raise an alarm as soon
as its mean has shifted down, while rarely alarming if nothing changed. The
two error rates trade off: a hair-trigger monitor detects fast but
false-alarms constantly; a sluggish one never cries wolf and never warns.

\subsection{The CUSUM statistic}
Page's one-sided CUSUM accumulates evidence of under-delivery but never lets
credit build up (the $\max(0,\cdot)$ clamp --- past overperformance must not
mask a current bleed):
\begin{equation}
C_0=0,\qquad
C_t=\max\big(0,\;C_{t-1}+(m-X_t)-k\big),\qquad
\text{alarm when } C_t>h .
\label{eq:cusum}
\end{equation}
Here $m$ is the promised edge, $k$ an \emph{allowance} (slack absorbing normal
noise; we use $k=0.5\hat\sigma$, the classic choice --- tuned to half the
shift one wants to detect, a rule inherited from the statistic's
sequential-likelihood-ratio derivation), and $h$ an \emph{alarm threshold}
($8\hat\sigma$) controlling the false-alarm rate: larger $h$ means rarer false
alarms but slower detection (formalised by \emph{average run lengths}: the
mean time to a false alarm on a healthy stream, ARL$_0$, versus the mean
detection delay after a real shift, ARL$_1$). The noise scale $\hat\sigma$ is
estimated on a \textbf{burn-in window} which is then \emph{excluded from
monitoring} --- you do not raise alarms on the data used to set the control
limits, a rule inherited from industrial quality control.

\begin{obsbox}
\textbf{Observation 16 (detection in three weeks, zero false alarms).} On the
steamroller desk the CUSUM crosses $h$ at \textbf{trade 83 of 1{,}467} ---
roughly three weeks into a six-year run, long before the equity curve is
visually diagnostic and \emph{years} before a Kupiec test would have a
meaningful sample. On the matched desk, the statistic breathes below threshold
for all 903 trades: no false alarm (Figure~\ref{fig:cusum}). The pairing
matters: a monitor is only as good as its false-alarm behaviour on healthy
systems, which is why both curves are shown on the same limits.
\end{obsbox}

\includegraphics[width=0.9\textwidth]{figs/fig7_cusum.png}
\caption{One-sided CUSUM on spot-scaled edge residuals (symlog $y$). Blue:
matched desk, never alarms. Red: mispriced desk, crosses the threshold at

\label{fig:cusum}
\end{figure}

\begin{keybox}
\textbf{The validator's playbook, assembled.} Coverage tests
(\S\ref{sec:varbacktest}) ask ``is the model's risk forecast honest?'';
clustering tests ask ``is the risk \emph{dynamics} right?''; bootstrap
inference (\S\ref{sec:bootstrap}) asks ``is the claimed edge real?''; CUSUM
(\S\ref{sec:cusum}) asks all of it \emph{continuously, in near-real-time}.
Together they detect the wrong model from outcomes alone --- no access to
``truth'' required --- with verified false-alarm control. That is the entire
logic of model risk management, demonstrated end to end on one desk.
\end{keybox}

\section{Limitations, stated plainly}
\label{sec:limitations}
\begin{itemize}[itemsep=2pt]
\item \textbf{Stylised microstructure.} One contract quoted at a time,
  Bernoulli arrivals, reduced-form elasticity \eqref{eq:elastic} --- no order
  book, no strategic clients, no inventory limits.
\item \textbf{Fixed truth within runs.} Each experiment's true world is a
  fixed-parameter Merton or GBM; real model risk also includes forms neither
  model spans (stochastic volatility, regime switching) and parameters that
  drift.
\item \textbf{Boundary null} in the LR test (\S\ref{sec:lrtest}) ---
  conservative here, and decisively overwhelmed at these statistic sizes.
\item \textbf{Incomplete-market economics compressed.} Jump-risk premia
  ($P$--$Q$ gaps) are real and only partially disentangled from hedging
  residuals in Observation 10; a full treatment would separate them.
\item \textbf{Data vintage.} Option chains end December 2025; NIFTY options
  are not in the free mirror (the repo ships a fetch script to extend both).
\end{itemize}
None of these threaten the qualitative conclusions; each is a concrete
extension path.

\section*{Glossary}
\begin{description}[itemsep=1pt,font=\normalfont\itshape]
\item[Adverse selection] Losing systematically to better-informed
  counterparties; here, mispriced quotes getting picked off.
\item[ATM/OTM/ITM] At/out-of/in-the-money: strike at, unfavourable to, or
  favourable to current spot.
\item[Delta] Sensitivity of option value to spot, $\partial V/\partial S$; the
  hedge ratio.
\item[Drift ($\mu$)] Expected growth rate of the underlying per year.
\item[Edge] Expected profit per trade; for a market maker, the half-spread.
\item[ES (Expected Shortfall)] Average loss beyond the VaR quantile.
\item[Half-spread ($s$)] Half the gap between ask and bid.
\item[Implied volatility] The $\sigma$ that makes Black--Scholes match a price.
\item[MLE] Maximum-likelihood estimator.
\item[Moneyness] $K/S$; locates strike relative to spot.
\item[$P$ vs $Q$ measure] Real-world vs risk-neutral (pricing) probabilities.
\item[Risk-free rate ($r$)] Return on riskless lending; the discounting rate.
\item[VaR] Loss threshold exceeded with probability $\alpha$.
\item[Vega] Sensitivity of option value to volatility.
\end{description}
